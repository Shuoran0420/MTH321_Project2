# MTH321 Project 2 — Presentation Script


## Slide 1 — Introduction
*Suggested time: 25 seconds*

Good morning, everyone.

We are Group C. Today, we are going to present our project, titled **2D Finite Element Analysis of a Microstrip Transmission Line**.

Our aim is not simply to calculate a numerical result, but also to demonstrate why that result can be trusted.

We will introduce our mathematical model, explain how we verified our numerical method, and discuss our main findings and limitations.

## Slide 2 — Problem and Engineering Goal
*Suggested time: 40 seconds*

First, let me explain the problem we are trying to solve.

Microstrip transmission lines are widely used in printed circuit boards. Their characteristic impedance is important because impedance mismatch can cause signal reflections.

In this project, our goal is to calculate the capacitance per unit length and use it to estimate the characteristic impedance.

However, obtaining a result close to fifty ohms is not enough.

We also need to consider numerical errors, especially those caused by the computational boundaries and the sharp corners of the conductor.

Therefore, we focus on both the final result and the numerical evidence supporting it.

## Slide 3 — Geometry and PDE Model
*Suggested time: 45 seconds*

This slide shows our physical model.

We consider a conducting strip above a ground plane, with a dielectric substrate between them and air above.

The substrate height is 1.6 millimetres, the strip width is 3 millimetres, and its thickness is 0.035 millimetres.

The relative permittivity of the substrate is 4.4.

We model the electrostatic potential using the generalized Laplace equation.

The signal conductor is fixed at one volt, while the ground plane is fixed at zero volts.

For the artificial top and side boundaries, we use homogeneous Neumann conditions.

Since the physical air region extends to infinity, we approximate it using a finite computational domain.

## Slide 4 — Weak Form and P1 FEM
*Suggested time: 45 seconds*

Next, we solve the mathematical model using the Finite Element Method.

More specifically, we use continuous piecewise-linear, or P1, triangular elements.

We first derive the weak formulation by multiplying the governing equation by a test function and integrating over the domain.

The important point is how we handle the interface between air and the dielectric substrate.

Although permittivity changes across this interface, the potential remains continuous.

The weak formulation also enforces flux balance in a weak sense, even though the discrete fluxes do not have to match point by point.

After applying the Dirichlet boundary conditions, we obtain a symmetric positive definite system, which we solve using a sparse direct solver.

## Slide 5 — Solver Verification: Smooth and Layered Problems
*Suggested time: 55 seconds*

Before trusting the microstrip results, we need to verify our solver.

We use two different tests.

The first is a smooth manufactured-solution test, where the exact solution is known.

For this test, the observed convergence order is approximately two in the L2 norm and one in the energy norm.

These results agree with the expected behaviour of P1 finite elements.

The second test considers two materials with different coefficients.

As shown in the figure, when the mesh is aligned with the material interface, the calculated normal flux agrees with the analytical solution almost to machine precision.

However, when the interface is not represented correctly, a significant flux error appears.

This is important because even a small algebraic residual does not necessarily mean that the physical model has been implemented correctly.

Together, these tests provide stronger evidence that our numerical implementation is reliable.

## Slide 6 — Domain Truncation and the 8H Choice
*Suggested time: 45 seconds*

Another important source of numerical error is domain truncation.

The real air region is infinite, but our computational domain must be finite.

To study this effect, we compare four domain sizes: 3H, 5H, 8H and 12H.

We use the 12H calculation as a finite-domain reference.

As we increase the domain size, the difference in calculated capacitance becomes smaller.

The 5H domain is the smallest tested domain with a difference below one percent.

However, we select 8H because its difference from the 12H reference is only about 0.182 percent.

We also perform a finer-mesh check on the 12H domain.

Importantly, the 0.182 percent is a sensitivity result from this domain study, not a strict error bound for our final M7 solution.

## Slide 7 — Corner Singularity and Uniform Convergence
*Suggested time: 40 seconds*

Next, we investigate mesh convergence.

The conductor has sharp corners, where the electric field can become very large.

This makes the numerical solution less smooth and can reduce the convergence rate.

For a smooth problem, we normally expect first-order convergence in the energy norm with P1 elements.

However, our uniform mesh study gives an observed rate of approximately 0.623.

This is consistent with reduced regularity near the conductor corners and material interface.

We also check how the fitted rate changes with the reference solution.

Therefore, we treat 0.623 as an observed rate over the tested coarse meshes, rather than an exact theoretical singularity exponent.

## Slide 8 — Why the Original Comparison Needed Correction
*Suggested time: 55 seconds*

Initially, we tried to improve accuracy by refining the mesh near the conductor corners.

However, during our numerical audit, we discovered a problem with the original error calculation.

We had assumed that nested coordinate lines would also produce nested triangular meshes.

But this is not always true.

As the diagram shows, a reference triangle may cross the edge of a coarse triangle.

Since the finite-element gradient can change across that edge, evaluating the error using only one point in the reference triangle may be inaccurate.

To correct this, we introduced triangle-overlay integration.

This method divides the overlapping regions so that the gradient difference can be integrated consistently.

This correction is an important part of our work because it makes the subsequent comparison between refinement methods more reliable.

## Slide 9 — Residual-Driven Adaptive Refinement
*Suggested time: 70 seconds*

After correcting the error calculation, we compare three approaches: uniform refinement, prescribed graded refinement, and residual-driven adaptive refinement.

The adaptive method uses a flux-jump indicator to identify where additional mesh resolution is needed.

Instead of refining the entire domain, it concentrates more elements in important regions.

At a relative energy-error target of 0.30, the interpolated results show that adaptive refinement requires approximately 224 degrees of freedom, compared with 273 for uniform refinement.

This represents a reduction of about 18 percent.

However, there is an important trade-off.

The adaptive algorithm requires repeated solving, error estimation, marking and refinement.

As a result, its cumulative computation time is about 1.83 times that of the uniform method in the original experiment.

We also examine how the error decreases as the number of degrees of freedom increases.

The measured slopes are approximately minus 0.362 overall and minus 0.247 over the later levels.

These results do not demonstrate recovery of the ideal minus one-half efficiency rate.

Therefore, our conclusion is that adaptive refinement improves the number of unknowns required at the selected error target, but does not necessarily reduce the total computational cost.

## Slide 10 — Final Capacitance Selection
*Suggested time: 50 seconds*

Now, we move to the final engineering calculation.

We use the uniform mesh sequence from M3 to M7 to examine capacitance convergence.

As the mesh becomes finer, the calculated capacitance decreases and begins to stabilise.

Our selected M7 result is 120.620 picofarads per metre.

The relative change between M6 and M7 is approximately 0.156 percent.

This is still above our preferred quality-control target of 0.1 percent.

A further M8 calculation was considered, but was not selected because of the expected computational resource requirements.

Instead, we examine the convergence trend and use Richardson extrapolation as an additional diagnostic.

We therefore accept M7 with a clearly stated remaining numerical sensitivity, rather than claiming perfect convergence.

## Slide 11 — Matched All-Air Solve and Effective Permittivity
*Suggested time: 40 seconds*

To calculate the characteristic impedance, we also need the capacitance when the dielectric material is replaced entirely by air.

An important requirement is that both calculations use exactly the same mesh.

This avoids introducing additional differences caused by remeshing.

Using the frozen M7 mesh, we obtain an all-air capacitance of 35.9682 picofarads per metre.

Dividing the dielectric capacitance by the all-air capacitance gives an effective relative permittivity of 3.3535.

This value is physically reasonable because it lies between the relative permittivities of air and the substrate.

## Slide 12 — Final Impedance and Engineering Benchmarks
*Suggested time: 40 seconds*

Using the two capacitance values, we calculate the characteristic impedance through the quasi-TEM relationship.

Our final FEM result is 50.642 ohms.

To check whether this value is reasonable, we compare it with two independent engineering approximations.

The Hammerstad–Jensen formula gives 50.201 ohms, while IPC-2141 gives 49.393 ohms.

Using Hammerstad–Jensen as the reference denominator, the relative discrepancy of our FEM result is approximately 0.88 percent.

The three results are reasonably close.

However, these engineering formulas are approximations, not exact solutions.

Therefore, their agreement supports the physical scale of our result, while our numerical verification and convergence studies provide the main evidence for its reliability.

## Slide 13 — Conclusions and Limitations
*Suggested time: 40 seconds*

To conclude, our project has developed and verified a two-dimensional finite-element model for a microstrip transmission line.

We verified the solver using both smooth and layered analytical problems.

We also identified and corrected a cross-mesh error-integration problem, allowing a more reliable comparison of refinement strategies.

Our final characteristic impedance is 50.642 ohms.

Adaptive refinement reduced the number of unknowns at the selected accuracy target, but did not reduce cumulative computation time.

Our recorded local reproduction also passed 58 regression tests and 208 numerical comparisons, although cross-platform reproduction has not yet been verified.

Finally, we recognise that our results still have numerical sensitivity, and that the quasi-static model does not include all real-world effects.

**Our main conclusion is that a numerical result becomes more credible when both its verification evidence and its limitations are clearly presented.**
