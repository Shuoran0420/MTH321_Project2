\begin{abstract}
Microstrip impedance can be estimated from the capacitance of its cross-section, but a finite-element result also depends on the outer computational boundary and how the field near conductor corners is resolved. We studied a finite-thickness microstrip trace using a two-dimensional electrostatic model with conforming triangular $P_1$ elements.

After verifying the solver with a manufactured solution, we examined domain truncation and mesh convergence. The uniform-mesh energy error had a fitted rate of $0.623$, below the smooth-problem rate of one. We initially tested coordinate-line grading near the corners, but an audit showed that the original cross-mesh error integration was not sufficiently reliable for precise efficiency claims. A separately tested triangle-overlay method was therefore used to compare uniform, graded and flux-jump adaptive refinement.

At a relative energy error of $0.30$, adaptivity needed approximately $224$ degrees of freedom (DOFs), compared with $273$ for uniform refinement and $349$ for grading. Its cumulative computation time was nevertheless higher.

The selected M7 solution gave a dielectric capacitance of $120.620\,\mathrm{pF/m}$. Reusing exactly the same mesh for the all-air calculation gave $35.9682\,\mathrm{pF/m}$, an effective permittivity of $3.3535$ and a characteristic impedance of $50.642\,\Omega$. Hammerstad--Jensen and IPC-2141 gave approximate impedance values of $50.201\,\Omega$ and $49.393\,\Omega$, respectively.

The results distinguish improved mesh efficiency from reduced total runtime while retaining explicit numerical limitations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A microstrip transmission line consists of a conducting signal trace above a conducting reference plane, separated by a dielectric substrate and surrounded by air. When the electrical length is not negligible compared with the relevant signal wavelengths, the characteristic impedance of the line controls reflection behaviour and therefore signal integrity. Under a lossless quasistatic or quasi-TEM model, this impedance can be inferred from the capacitance per unit length of the cross-section and the corresponding all-air capacitance\footnote{For the lossless transmission-line and microstrip model, see D. M. Pozar, \emph{Microwave Engineering}, 4th ed., Wiley, 2011. The project-specific capacitance-pair identities are stated in the supplied \emph{Capacitor2d} specification, Appendix A.}. The report structure follows the project requirement that numerical claims be supported by verification, convergence evidence, cost information, and reproducible artefacts.

We focused on two main questions: how reliable the calculated microstrip impedance is, and whether local mesh refinement can improve computational efficiency. To answer these questions, we first verified the FEM solver and studied domain and mesh convergence. We then compared uniform, graded and adaptive refinement using energy error, degrees of freedom, matrix nonzeros and measured computation time.

The nominal geometry is a copper trace of width $w=3.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and thickness $t=0.035\,\mathrm{mm}$ on a substrate of height $H=1.6\,\mathrm{mm}$ and relative permittivity $\epsr=4.4$. All computations use SI units internally. The finite computational domain has $x\in[-W,W]$ and $y\in[0,H_{\mathrm{air}}]$; the final engineering domain uses $W/H=H_{\mathrm{air}}/H=8$ after a separate domain study.

\subsection{Geometry and material model}
The substrate occupies $0<y<H$ and has $\epsr=4.4$. The air region is above the substrate and has $\epsr=1$. The signal conductor is centred at $x=0$ and occupies $H\le y\le H+t$ and $-w/2\le x\le w/2$. Its interior is removed from the field domain, so its boundary is an internal Dirichlet boundary rather than a material subregion.

\subsection{Governing equation and boundary conditions}
The electrostatic energy model is described by Hiptmair~\cite[Sec.~1.2.2]{hiptmair2025}. For an admissible potential, the stored energy per unit length is
\[
W_e(\phi)=\frac12\int_\Omega\varepsilon(x,y)|\nabla\phi|^2\,d\Omega.
\]
With the conductor potentials fixed, its stationarity gives the following divergence-form equation. The permittivity is a material property, so it is piecewise constant here: $4.4\varepsilon_0$ in the substrate and $\varepsilon_0$ in air. Hackbusch~\cite[Sec.~10.1]{hackbusch2017} explains why such positive discontinuous coefficients are naturally treated in a variational formulation, with separate integration over the material regions.

The potential $\phi$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 -\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon \nabla \phi)=0
 \qquad \text{in }\Omega,
 \label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_0\epsr$ is piecewise constant. The essential boundary conditions are
\[
\phi=V_0 \quad \text{on the signal trace},\qquad
\phi=0 \quad \text{on the ground plane}.
\]
The artificial top and side boundaries use the homogeneous Neumann condition
\[
\varepsilon \nabla \phi\cdot n=0.
\]
This same far-boundary convention is used throughout the domain-truncation sequence. Across the air--substrate interface, the exact electrostatic interface conditions are continuity of potential and balance of normal displacement flux.

\section{Finite Element Formulation}
Let $\Gamma_D$ denote the union of the trace and ground Dirichlet boundaries. The affine trial space is
\[
V_g=\{u\in H^1(\Omega):u=V_0 \text{ on the trace},\ u=0 \text{ on the ground}\},
\]
and the homogeneous test space is
\[
\mathcal V=\{v\in H^1(\Omega):v=0 \text{ on }\Gamma_D\}.
\]
Here $V_0$ denotes the prescribed trace voltage, while $\mathcal V$ denotes the homogeneous function space. Multiplying~\eqref{eq:pde} by $v\in\mathcal V$ and integrating by parts gives
\[
\int_{\Omega} \varepsilon \nabla\phi\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega
-\int_{\partial\Omega} \varepsilon \nabla\phi\cdot n\,v\,ds=0.
\]
The Dirichlet part of the boundary term vanishes because $v=0$ there, and the artificial Neumann term is zero by construction. The weak problem is therefore:
\[
\text{find }\phi\in V_g\text{ such that }
\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon \nabla\phi\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega=0
\quad\forall v\in\mathcal V.
\]

\subsection{Well-posedness and discrete stability}
The finite field domain is a connected bounded polygonal Lipschitz domain with the trace hole removed. Its ground and trace boundaries give $\Gamma_D$ positive boundary measure. For
\[
a(u,v)=\int_\Omega\varepsilon\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega,
\qquad \varepsilon_0\le\varepsilon(x)\le4.4\varepsilon_0,
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz and the positive lower coefficient bound give
\begin{equation}
|a(u,v)|\le4.4\varepsilon_0\|\nabla u\|_{L^2}\|\nabla v\|_{L^2},
\qquad a(v,v)\ge\varepsilon_0\|\nabla v\|_{L^2}^2.
\label{eq:coercivity}
\end{equation}
For $v\in\mathcal V$, the Poincar\'e inequality
$\|v\|_{L^2}\le C_P\|\nabla v\|_{L^2}$ holds because the Dirichlet boundary is nonempty with positive measure. Thus the gradient seminorm is a norm on $\mathcal V$, equivalent to its $H^1$ norm, and $a$ is continuous and coercive. The dielectric jump preserves these properties because both materials have strictly positive permittivity.

Choose an $H^1$ lifting $g$ of the prescribed trace and ground values and write $\phi=g+u$. The homogeneous problem is $a(u,v)=-a(g,v)$ for all $v\in\mathcal V$. Its bounded right-hand side and coercivity give existence and uniqueness by the Lax--Milgram theorem. In particular, the difference $w$ of two solutions satisfies $a(w,w)=0$, hence $\nabla w=0$; connectedness makes $w$ constant and its zero Dirichlet trace forces $w=0$. The homogeneous Neumann outer boundary therefore does not leave the constant nullspace of a pure-Neumann problem.

The same estimate applies to the conforming subspace $\mathcal V_h\subset\mathcal V$. For a nonzero vector $z$ of free coefficients with associated function $v_h$, 
\[
z^T K_{FF}z=a(v_h,v_h)>0.
\]
Consequently, the reduced matrix is symmetric positive definite. For a load perturbation $\delta\ell$ with unchanged Dirichlet data, testing the difference equation by $\delta u_h$ gives
\[
\|\nabla\delta u_h\|_{L^2}\le
\varepsilon_0^{-1}\|\delta\ell\|_{\mathcal V'},\qquad
\|\delta\ell\|_{\mathcal V'}=
\sup_{v\in\mathcal V\setminus\{0\}}
\frac{|\delta\ell(v)|}{\|\nabla v\|_{L^2}}.
\]
This is stability in a function-space norm on the fixed domain, not a mesh-independent Euclidean matrix condition number. It also does not imply a first-order energy convergence rate: approximation rates depend on solution regularity and mesh resolution. The measured $p_E\simeq0.623$ is compatible with a stable problem whose corner/interface field is less regular than the smooth verification solution.

The well-posedness argument follows the standard variational theory for elliptic problems discussed by Hackbusch~\cite[Sec.~7.2]{hackbusch2017} and Quarteroni~\cite[Secs.~3.4 and 4.2]{quarteroni2017}. In our model, the permittivity is discontinuous across the air--substrate interface, but it remains strictly positive in both regions. Therefore, the discontinuity does not prevent the weak problem from being coercive. Quarteroni's Galerkin discussion also explains the positive-definite stiffness matrix obtained after restricting to the homogeneous discrete test space.

\subsection{Assembly, solution and capacitance extraction}

The discrete space uses conforming triangular $P_1$ elements, as developed by Quarteroni~\cite[Sec.~4.5]{quarteroni2017} and Hiptmair~\cite[Sec.~2.4]{hiptmair2025}. Local element assembly and essential-boundary elimination follow the standard procedures described by Hiptmair~\cite[Secs.~2.7.4 and 2.7.6]{hiptmair2025}. Writing
\[
\phi_h=\sum_j \Phi_j N_j,
\]
the stiffness matrix has entries
\[
K_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon \nabla N_i\cdot \nabla N_j\,d\Omega.
\]
Because $\varepsilon$ is discontinuous at the material interface, the integral is evaluated element by element using each triangle's material tag. In a conforming $H^1$ finite-element space, $\phi_h$ is continuous across the air--substrate interface. Since the discontinuous coefficient is integrated in the same global weak form, the interface flux-balance condition is enforced automatically in the weak or distributional sense. No separate Lagrange multiplier is required. This does not mean that the two elementwise $P_1$ normal-flux traces $\varepsilon\nabla\phi_h\cdot n$ are pointwise identical on every interface edge.

The recorded environment uses scikit-fem 12.0.2 for mesh topology and the manufactured-solution basis, quadrature and assembly. In the microstrip solver, project code evaluates the constant $P_1$ basis gradients explicitly and assembles $K^K_{ij}=\varepsilon_K|K|\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j$ into a SciPy sparse matrix. This expression integrates the element stiffness exactly because the coefficient and gradients are constant on each material-conforming triangle. SciPy supplies the sparse direct solve. Project code controls the trace hole, material and boundary tags, refinement, capacitance extraction and validation; these choices are checked separately from the library interfaces.

Dirichlet conditions are imposed by eliminating the trace and ground degrees of freedom, leaving a sparse symmetric positive definite free-degree system solved by a sparse direct solver.

The capacitance is extracted in two ways. The energy method is
\[
W_e=\frac12\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon |\nabla \phi_h|^2\,d\Omega,\qquad
C_E=\frac{2W_e}{V_0^2}.
\]
The reaction-charge method sums the assembled Dirichlet reactions on the trace:
\[
C_Q=\frac{|Q_{\mathrm{trace}}|}{|V_0|}.
\]
Agreement between $C_E$ and $C_Q$ checks consistency of energy integration and reaction extraction. Geometry and boundary-tag tests are also required, since a shared modelling error could affect both quantities. Spatial accuracy is assessed through refinement.

\medskip
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Microstrip $P_1$ FEM solver}\label{alg:fem}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Material-conforming mesh $\mathcal T_h$, geometry, $\varepsilon_K$ and voltage $V_0$
\State Generate the study mesh, or load the exact frozen mesh for a matched replay.
\State Verify the trace hole, interface, element regions and trace/ground/far boundary sets.
\State Set $D=D_{\rm trace}\cup D_{\rm ground}$, $F=\{\text{all nodes}\}\setminus D$.
\State Initialise $\Phi_D=V_0$ on the trace and $\Phi_D=0$ on ground; set $K=0$.
\For{each triangle $K_e\in\mathcal T_h$}
\State Compute $|K_e|$ and its three constant basis gradients.
\State Assemble $\varepsilon_{K_e}|K_e|\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j$ into the sparse matrix.
\EndFor
\State Solve $K_{FF}\Phi_F=-K_{FD}\Phi_D$ by the sparse direct solver.
\State Evaluate $\nabla\phi_h$, $W_e$, $C_E=2W_e/V_0^2$ and reactions $r=K\Phi$.
\State Sum trace reactions to obtain $Q_{\rm trace}$ and $C_Q=|Q_{\rm trace}|/|V_0|$.
\State Check bounds, symmetry, residual, charge balance and $C_E/C_Q$ consistency.
\Ensure Potential, capacitances and diagnostics; failed checks disqualify interpretation.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\medskip
Algorithm~\ref{alg:fem} separates the physical boundary specification from the linear solve. The homogeneous Neumann edges add no load, whereas prescribed voltages enter through $-K_{FD}\Phi_D$. Reactions must be recovered from the full assembled matrix, before its boundary rows are discarded. The implementation is in \path{code/fem_solver.py} and \path{code/capacitance.py}.

\section{Solver Verification and Baseline Checks}
\subsection{Manufactured-solution verification}
Known-solution tests make the discretisation error measurable and allow observed rates to be compared with the smooth-solution estimates. This is the code-validation strategy discussed by Hiptmair~\cite[Sec.~3.8]{hiptmair2025}, with the expected finite-element energy and $L^2$ estimates given by Quarteroni~\cite[Sec.~4.5]{quarteroni2017}. Matching the rates supports the tested implementation; it does not by itself verify every feature of the microstrip model.

Before using the microstrip result, the implementation was verified on a smooth known solution. The test problem uses a unit square with zero Dirichlet data and exact solution
\[
u(x,y)=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
\]
The manufactured forcing and boundary data are explicitly
\[
-\Delta u=f,\qquad f(x,y)=2\pi^2\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y),\qquad
u|_{\partial(0,1)^2}=0.
\]
Here the named errors are $\|u-u_h\|_{L^2(\Omega)}$ and
$\|u-u_h\|_E=(\int_\Omega|\nabla(u-u_h)|^2\,d\Omega)^{1/2}$, with coefficient one for this verification problem. The expected smooth $P_1$ rates are two and one, respectively. The five consecutive meshes below contain the required $h,h/2,h/4,h/8$ protocol and one further level $h/16$. Pairwise orders are
$p_i=\log(E_{i-1}/E_i)/\log(h_{i-1}/h_i)$.

The Problem Pack supplies a second check aimed specifically at discontinuous coefficients. On the dimensionless unit square, set $a=1$ for $x<1/2$ and $a=10$ for $x>1/2$, with $f=0$ and exact Dirichlet data on the entire boundary:

The test calls the production \texttt{assemble\_stiffness} routine with relative coefficients 1 and 10, then divides out the common $\varepsilon_0$ to obtain the dimensionless problem. Strong Dirichlet elimination is applied with the analytic boundary values. Because the fitted exact solution belongs to the continuous piecewise-affine space, errors should be at round-off; a convergence slope fitted to these tiny errors would have no useful meaning.

\subsection{Baseline microstrip sanity checks}
The representative baseline solve passed the expected physical and algebraic checks. The potential remained in the interval $[0,1]$, the symmetry error about $x=0$ was near round-off, the reduced matrix symmetry error was zero to numerical precision, and the residual was negligible. The energy and reaction capacitances agreed to within $2.54\times10^{-15}$ relative difference.

\paragraph{Linear solver and algebraic-error control.}
After Dirichlet elimination, the free system is sparse, symmetric and positive definite, so a sparse direct solve is appropriate for the tested problem sizes. This choice also avoids ambiguity about iterative stopping tolerances. A small residual alone would not prove negligible algebraic error, so the residual is interpreted together with conditioning diagnostics. For the representative baseline solve, the estimated physical reduced-system condition number was $1.7922363\times10^5$, while the diagonal-scaled diagnostic was $1.4249857\times10^4$. The free residual infinity norm was $3.101927\times10^{-25}$. The scaled diagnostic is not the physical condition number, but these conditioning and backward-residual diagnostics indicate that algebraic solve error is far below the observed finite-element discretisation effects.

\section{Mesh Convergence and Corner Singularity}
\subsection{Corner-field behaviour}
Finite-element error estimates depend on solution regularity as well as the approximation space. Hackbusch~\cite[Secs.~8.5 and 9.1]{hackbusch2017} and Hiptmair~\cite[Sec.~3.4]{hiptmair2025} discuss this dependence and the effect of nonsmooth domains. These results explain why a smooth-problem rate need not apply here; they do not predict the measured value $0.623$.

The conductor corners create singular electric-field behaviour. The lower trace corners are especially delicate because the conductor boundary meets the air--substrate interface. A mesh-dependent peak field that increases under refinement is therefore consistent with singular behaviour; it is not itself an accuracy norm.

\begin{figure}[H]

\subsection{Uniform energy-norm convergence}
Uniform convergence was measured against an over-refined nested reference $R2=M6$. The error norm was
\[
\|e\|_E^2=\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon |\nabla(\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}-\phi_h)|^2\,d\Omega.
\]
The relative energy error used in the figures and tables is
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{rel}}^{\mathrm{ref}}=
\frac{\|\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}-\phi_h\|_E}{\|\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_E},\qquad
\|\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_E^2=\int_\Omega\varepsilon|\nabla\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}|^2\,d\Omega.
\label{eq:relative-energy}
\end{equation}
When the reference changes, both numerator and denominator use that same reference; this dimensionless ratio is not a relative capacitance difference.
Coefficient vectors on different meshes were never directly subtracted. Instead, the coarse $P_1$ gradient was evaluated on the reference mesh elements and the error was integrated on the reference grid.

\section{Historical Graded Refinement and Integration Limitation}
\label{sec:historical-graded}
We tested whether concentrating coordinate-line refinement near the four trace corners could improve efficiency, with additional emphasis on the lower conductor/material-junction corners. The method remained symmetric about $x=0$ and used the same physical model as the uniform sequence.

\paragraph{Reproducible grading rule.}
The shared base coordinate sets, in units of $H$, are
\[
B_x/H=(-8,-2,-0.9375,0,0.9375,2,8),\qquad
B_y/H=(0,1,1.021875,1.5,8).
\]
Let $D_k(B)$ bisect every interval of $B$ exactly $k$ times (ratio $1/2$). For parameters $(b,u,\ell)$, retain the background coordinates $D_b(B_x)$ and $D_b(B_y)$. Add coordinates from $D_\ell(B_x)$ within $0.45H$ of either $x=\pm w/2$ and from $D_\ell(B_y)$ within $0.45H$ of $y=H$. Add coordinates from $D_u(B_x)$ within $0.30H$ of either $x=\pm w/2$ and from $D_u(B_y)$ within $0.30H$ of $y=H+t$. Take sorted unions, snap to the M6 coordinate set at $10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}$ precision, form the Cartesian grid, and split each retained rectangle along its bottom-left/top-right diagonal; remove the trace interior. These are axis-aligned bands, not circular element-distance zones: adding a coordinate line also spends resolution away from a corner.

\paragraph{Why the comparison was repeated.}
The coordinate-subset assumption did not ensure nested triangles. For G2, $21440$ of the $180224$ reference triangles cross a coarse diagonal, covering about $4.55\%$ of field-domain area. This is not an error bound. We retained the original results, kept the rejecting safety guard, and repeated the comparison using triangle-overlay integration in Section~\ref{sec:residual-adaptive}. Appendix~A, case audit~3 records the geometric test, replacement method and exact evidence locations.

As discussed by Hiptmair~\cite[Sec.~3.5]{hiptmair2025}, numerical quadrature can introduce additional errors into a finite-element calculation. In our original graded-mesh comparison, some reference triangles crossed coarse-element boundaries where the gradient was discontinuous. We therefore replaced the centroid-based error integration with triangle-overlay integration before making quantitative efficiency comparisons. Here the approximation affected error-norm post-processing, rather than the production stiffness assembly. The crossed-triangle count, $4.55\%$ area fraction and corrected results are our own measurements, not conclusions taken from the text.

The initial lack of improvement also led us to test another refinement approach: choosing elements from the computed flux-jump residual rather than prescribing coordinate bands around the corners.

\section{Limitations}
The physical model is electrostatic and quasistatic/quasi-TEM. It does not include dielectric dispersion, dielectric loss, conductor loss, surface roughness, solder-mask loading, radiation, anisotropy, or manufacturing tolerances. The trace and ground are perfect conductors.

The numerical result also has limitations. The $8H$ domain is justified by a finite $12H$ comparison, not by an exact infinite-domain solution. The final M7 mesh was accepted through a resource-aware asymptotic fallback rather than by directly reaching the preferred $0.1\%$ capacitance-change target. Richardson extrapolation is diagnostic only. The historical graded curves use approximate cross-mesh quadrature; only the separately recomputed exact-overlay extension is used for current quantitative efficiency claims. The adaptive comparison uses one residual indicator, a primary D\"orfler parameter ($\theta=0.5$) with a separate $0.3/0.7$ sensitivity study, one symmetry/conformity closure and two declared starting meshes. It does not establish optimality among estimators or marking strategies; the indicator-to-error ratio has not stabilised.

\section{Conclusions}
For the studied microstrip geometry, the selected M7 solution gives $C=120.620\,\mathrm{pF/m}$. The all-air solve on exactly the same mesh gives $\Cair=35.9682\,\mathrm{pF/m}$, leading to $\epseff=3.3535$ and $\Zzero=50.642\,\Omega$. This is close to the common $50\,\Omega$ design target.

At relative energy error $0.30$, residual-based adaptivity needs approximately $224$ DOFs, compared with $273$ for uniform refinement and $349$ for grading. It uses fewer DOFs and matrix nonzeros, but its cumulative time is $13.1$ ms compared with $7.15$ ms for uniform refinement. The preceding solve--estimate--mark--refine cycles add work that a one-shot uniform calculation does not require. Thus, fewer unknowns did not mean a shorter total runtime in this comparison.

The M6--M7 spatial indicator is approximately $0.156\%$, while the separate, coarser matched-core domain study gives $0.182\%$. Neither is a rigorous error bound, and the latter is not a measured M7 truncation error. M7 was accepted through the documented resource/asymptotic fallback, and Richardson extrapolation remains diagnostic. The quasi-static model also omits losses and full-wave dispersion or radiation, so the reported impedance should be interpreted within those assumptions.
